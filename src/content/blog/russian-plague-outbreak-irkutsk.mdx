> **Quick answer:** There is no confirmed plague outbreak in Russia as of 7 October 2026. A 28-year-old worker at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Institute died in hospital in early October after severe pneumonia, and Russian media reported pneumonic plague, but Russian federal authorities say her cause of death was "pneumonia of unknown origin" and have told the World Health Organization (WHO) that no plague case was recorded. A reported second death has not been officially confirmed. Plague is curable with common antibiotics if treated early, and WHO rates the risk to Europe as very low.

Headlines about "plague in Russia" spread quickly this week, and many of them went further than the facts. A young laboratory worker died in Siberia, a hospital was partly closed, and nearly 200 people were placed under observation. That is a serious event and it deserves close attention.

But what has been officially confirmed is much narrower than what has been reported. This explainer sets out what happened in Irkutsk, what Russian officials and WHO have actually said, what plague is, and whether people in the UK or elsewhere have any reason to worry. This is a fast-moving story and details may change.

---

## Is there a plague outbreak in Russia?

No outbreak has been confirmed. One person has died, and several Russian outlets reported the cause as pneumonic plague. Russian federal health authorities have not accepted that.

Russia's health watchdog, **Rospotrebnadzor**, said the woman was diagnosed with "pneumonia of unknown aetiology" and that expanded testing found no microorganisms linked to her work. On 6 October, Russian authorities told WHO that tests on her contacts found no dangerous infectious diseases. WHO chief Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said Russia reported that no plague case had been registered in Irkutsk region.

At the same time, WHO says no cause of death has been officially confirmed yet. So the honest answer is: plague is suspected by many, denied by Moscow, and not proven either way in public.

---

## What happened in Irkutsk?

The woman, a laboratory technician named in Russian media as Darya Shipilova, worked at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute of Siberia and the Far East. She was admitted to hospital in Shelekhov, a town near Irkutsk, with severe pneumonia around 29 September and died overnight on 1–2 October.

From 1 October, the Shelekhov district hospital stopped admitting and discharging patients in several departments, and its inpatient unit was placed under quarantine. The outpatient clinic stayed open.

![Specialized microbiological research laboratory at an infectious disease research institute](/images/irkutsk-anti-plague-institute-lab.jpg)

On 2 October, Irkutsk Governor Igor Kobzev said a regional resident was suspected of having a "particularly dangerous infection" and held an emergency meeting attended by Rospotrebnadzor head Anna Popova. He said all known contacts were under medical observation, had no signs of illness and had tested negative.

Reports said 197 contacts were identified and 189 of them were hospitalised for observation. Independent outlets also reported that dozens of staff at the institute were isolated.

---

## Has a second person died?

Russian media reported on 6 October that a second person had died at a hospital in Irkutsk region after allegedly contracting plague. This has **not been officially confirmed**. No laboratory results or official statements have linked any second death to plague.

On 7 October, WHO said Russia must clarify conflicting reports about the first death and whether there is an additional case. Until Russian authorities or WHO confirm otherwise, treat the second death as an unverified report.

---

## How did she get infected?

Nobody knows yet, and no explanation has been officially confirmed.

Several theories have circulated:
- One regional outlet reported that she broke a test tube containing plague bacteria at work in late September.
- Other reports said she had recently returned from a work trip near the Mongolian border.
- Another report said she had returned from Thailand. Officials in neighbouring Buryatia said she had not visited their region.

Rospotrebnadzor says it found no evidence of any accident involving pathogens at the institute. Russian media have reported that investigators opened a criminal case into possible safety violations, but this does not establish a cause.

Russia's anti-plague institutes date back to Soviet times. They monitor plague in wild animals, test samples and study the bacteria from natural plague areas in Siberia, the Altai and Mongolia. Staff at such labs are among the few groups WHO says may be offered plague vaccination.

## What is bubonic plague?

Plague is caused by a bacterium called *Yersinia pestis*, which lives naturally in small mammals such as rodents and the fleas that feed on them.

Bubonic plague is the most common form. It usually starts with a bite from an infected flea. WHO lists the symptoms as sudden fever, chills, head and body aches, weakness, nausea and vomiting, along with painful swollen lymph nodes, often in the groin, armpit or neck. Symptoms usually appear one to seven days after infection.

It is treated with antibiotics, and spread between people is rare.

---

## What is pneumonic plague?

Pneumonic plague is plague in the lungs. It can develop when bubonic plague spreads, or when someone breathes in droplets from a person who already has it.

It is the most dangerous form for two reasons:
1. It can pass from person to person through respiratory droplets.
2. It moves fast: WHO says symptoms can appear in under 24 hours and include shortness of breath and coughing, sometimes with blood. Untreated, it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of symptoms starting.

The good news is that WHO says recovery rates are high when it is diagnosed and treated within 24 hours of symptoms.

---

## Bubonic vs pneumonic vs septicaemic plague

| Form | How it spreads | Main symptoms | Fatality if untreated | Treatable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bubonic** | Infected flea bites; contact with infected animals | Fever, chills, aches, painful swollen lymph nodes | 30% to 60% (WHO) | Yes, with antibiotics |
| **Pneumonic** | Respiratory droplets from an infected person; can develop from other forms | Fever, cough, shortness of breath, sometimes bloody sputum | Almost always fatal (WHO) | Yes, if treated very early |
| **Septicaemic** | Bacteria multiply in the blood; can develop from other forms | Fever, bleeding under the skin, organ failure | Almost always fatal (WHO) | Yes, if treated early |

## Is plague curable?

**Yes.** Plague is a bacterial infection and WHO says antibiotics work well against it, so early diagnosis and treatment save lives. Common antibiotics can cure even pneumonic plague if they are given early.

The danger lies in delay. Because the pneumonic form progresses so quickly, doctors are advised to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected rather than wait for lab confirmation.

---

## Is plague contagious?

It depends on the form. Bubonic plague is not normally passed from one person to another; WHO describes human-to-human spread as rare. Pneumonic plague is different: it can spread through respiratory droplets during close contact, which is why contacts in Irkutsk were isolated and tested.

## Should people in the UK or elsewhere worry?

Based on current evidence, **no**. WHO's initial assessment on 6 October rated the risk as moderate to low for Irkutsk, low for Russia as a whole and very low for the WHO European region, which includes the UK. WHO says it will update this as more information comes in.

The UK Foreign Office (FCDO) already advises against all travel to Russia because of the war in Ukraine. As of 7 October 2026, its Russia advice did not include any plague-specific warning. The US State Department says it is monitoring the situation with the CDC.

Anyone who feels unwell after travel to an affected area should contact a doctor or call NHS 111.

---

## Where does plague still exist today?

Plague survives in wild animals on every inhabited continent except Oceania, according to WHO. Since the 1990s, most human cases have been in Africa. Between 2019 and 2025, most cases reported to WHO came from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Madagascar, where bubonic plague is seen almost every year between September and April.

Russia has natural plague areas too, including mountain zones in the Altai, Tuva and the Caucasus. Over the border, Mongolia has identified 137 natural plague foci, some near Russia. Marmots are a known carrier there, and human cases in Mongolia have been linked to hunting or eating them. Russia's last recorded human case was in 2016, when a 10-year-old boy in the Altai Republic fell ill.

## People Also Ask

### What country has the most bubonic plague cases?

According to WHO, most human plague cases reported between 2019 and 2025 came from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Madagascar. Madagascar sees bubonic plague cases almost every year, mainly between September and April.

### How fatal is pneumonic plague?

Untreated pneumonic plague is almost always fatal and can kill within 18 to 24 hours of symptoms starting, according to WHO. With antibiotics given early, ideally within 24 hours of symptoms, most people recover.

### What killed more, plague or COVID?

Historically, plague. WHO says the Black Death caused more than 50 million deaths in Europe in the 14th century. COVID-19 has just over 7 million officially reported deaths worldwide, although WHO estimated about 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020 and 2021 alone. Today, plague kills very few people because it is treatable.

### Is bubonic plague 100% fatal?

No. Even without treatment, WHO puts the death rate for bubonic plague at 30% to 60%. With prompt antibiotics, the large majority of patients survive.
